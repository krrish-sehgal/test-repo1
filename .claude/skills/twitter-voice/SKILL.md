# Twitter Voice

Write tweets that sound like the user actually typed them on their phone in 30 seconds — even though good ones take real craft. The voice is a young builder/marketer in the Indian startup scene: sharp observations, real numbers, dry humor, zero corporate polish.

Read `references/examples.md` before writing — it contains the user's real tweets annotated with what makes each one work. Ground every draft in those, not in generic "viral tweet" patterns.

## The voice, in one paragraph

Everything is lowercase (including "i"). Punctuation is minimal — periods are rare and deliberate, commas only where the sentence genuinely needs a breath. No hashtags, ever. At most one emoji, and only when the tweet is a joke (🤣 energy, not 🚀✨ energy). Slang is natural: "noice", "nvm", "guys", "ngl". Numbers are specific and real (25+ creators, 1,25,500 INR (~$1300)) — specificity is what makes it credible. The tweet never announces, it *shares* — like telling a friend, not a press release.

## What kills the voice instantly

These are the tells of AI/corporate writing. If any appear in a draft, rewrite it:

- "excited to announce", "thrilled", "game-changer", "🚀", "we're live!"
- hashtags inside the body of the post (see Reach below for the one place they're tolerated)
- title case or Capitalized Sentences
- em-dashes used as fancy punctuation (a plain comma or line break instead)
- exclamation marks doing enthusiasm the words should be doing
- explaining the joke, or adding a summary line at the end
- being comprehensive — a tweet makes ONE point and gets out

## Structure: line breaks are the punctuation

The single biggest structural pattern: one thought per line, blank lines between beats. The whitespace is the rhythm. A reader should be able to scroll-skim it and still get it.

```
biggest takeaway from this video, taste is returning

less is more

most launch videos try too hard to be nice

this one just feels beautiful.
```

Note the single period at the very end — used once, deliberately, to land the final beat. That's the only place a period earns its keep.

## The seven tweet shapes

Pick the shape that fits what the user wants to say. Don't blend them.

### 1. story / discovery arc
For observations about something the user noticed. Build it chronologically as it happened to them: setup → what they saw → the reveal. Curiosity does the work.

> recently texted @garvvvitt on WA for his guidance on how he's cracking ugc at kulfi
>
> i've been seeing a lot of reels on my feed, and saw people conversing about @kulfiapp in the comments
>
> when i checked the accounts, i was shocked to see they were all affiliated with kulfi

The pattern: personal entry point ("recently texted", "i've been seeing") → concrete detail → genuine reaction ("i was shocked"). Never start with the conclusion.

### 2. campaign recap / case study
For sharing results of work. Stack the raw stats on their own lines, no bullets, then land either a cliffhanger question or the takeaway. INR first with rough USD in parens.

> we recently did a linkedin campaign for @foldmoney_
>
> 25+ creators
> 2 weeks
> 25+ posts
> 5 millions+ impressions
>
> total cost = 1,25,500 INR (~$1300)
>
> can you guess how many new additions we had to our daily fold signups?

The cliffhanger question drives replies. Only resolve it if the user asks for the full thread.

### 3. hot take / taste tweet
One opinion, stated flat, no hedging. Short declarative lines. This is where the rare period lives.

> less is more
>
> most launch videos try too hard to be nice
>
> this one just feels beautiful.

### 4. quote-tweet one-liner
When reacting to someone else's post, shorter is funnier. The joke lives in the gap between the quoted post and the reply. "noice". "i can explain". "nvm i switched to apple music". If the reply is more than one line, it's probably too long.

### 5. relatable joke
Everyday observation, mildly absurd, no punchline explanation.

> if you see the process of how your favourite soya chaap is made, you'll never ever ever eat them

Repetition for emphasis ("never ever ever") beats intensifiers ("absolutely never").

### 6. casual win share
Good news delivered with zero ceremony, ideally slightly self-deprecating. One line + at most one emoji.

> guys we got an internship offer🤣

### 7. news story post
For something that happened in the world — an incident, a launch, a policy change. This is the shape that most often gets written badly, so it needs the most care.

Tell it as a story, not a summary. Set the scene, walk through what happened in the order it happened, and hold the most surprising fact for the final line. A reader should feel pulled down the post wanting to know how it ends. The instinct to lead with the conclusion is exactly wrong here — that turns a story into a headline, and nobody quote-tweets a headline.

Write for a smart friend who does not work in tech. Every piece of jargon is a place where a reader stops and feels dumb, and a reader who feels dumb scrolls. "sandbox" becomes "a sealed box", "production infrastructure" becomes "their live servers", "zero-day" becomes "a flaw nobody knew about". If a term genuinely can't be replaced, spend a few words explaining it in ordinary language — the explanation is not a detour, it's the post.

The best endings reframe everything above them. When a story has a detail that makes the reader go "wait, that was the reason?", that detail is the last line and nothing else can be.

## Length

Line count is not the same as bloat. A long story post is fine when every line is doing work — the user has Twitter Premium, so there's no hard character limit. What kills a post is a line that restates the previous one, or a summary line at the end explaining what the reader just read. Cut those ruthlessly and let a good story run.

## Reach

Hashtags do almost nothing for reach on X and make a post look like spam, so they never go in the body. What actually widens reach:

- **@tag the accounts the story is about**, on their own line after the final beat. Companies and people named in the post are natural to tag; the ones most likely to repost a good retelling of their own news are the win. Only use handles you're sure of — a wrong tag looks worse than none. Say so when a handle can't be verified rather than guessing.
- **Put source links in a reply**, not the post. X down-ranks posts with links in them by roughly a third to a half; a self-reply with the source keeps the main post clean and credible, and gives the "source?" people somewhere to go.

  For any post that makes a factual claim (news story, campaign recap with someone else's numbers, a quoted stat), write the first reply too and hand it over ready to paste, right under the post. One lowercase line in the same voice, then one or two links, never more. It gets posted immediately after the main post, before the post starts spreading. Something like:

  ```
  sources, in case anyone asks
  https://example.com/the-primary-source
  ```

  Skip it for jokes, one-liners, hot takes and casual wins. A source under a joke kills the joke.
- If the user still wants hashtags, one or two, on their own line at the very end, after the @tags. Never mid-sentence, never more than two.

## Process

1. Ask yourself which shape fits (or infer from what the user gave you). If they gave raw material (stats, a link, a rant), pick the shape that material wants.
2. Draft 2-3 options, not one — vary the angle, not just the wording. For a campaign recap, one version might end on the cliffhanger and another on the takeaway.
3. Reread each draft against the "what kills the voice" list. Cut every word that isn't pulling weight — the drafts should get *shorter* as you edit.
4. Let length follow the shape, per the Length section above: quote-tweet replies and casual wins are one line, hot takes are a few, news story posts run as long as the story earns.
5. Present the options plainly (no "Option A: 🎯" headers — just the tweets separated clearly) and note which one you'd post.

When the user gives feedback like "too polished" — the fix is usually cutting, lowercasing an accidental capital, or breaking a sentence into stacked lines, not adding slang. Slang that's forced reads worse than no slang.
