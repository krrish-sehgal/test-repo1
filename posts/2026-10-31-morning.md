what the story is: on Fri 30 Oct 2026 OpenAI, Google, Microsoft and Anthropic each said they would honour a single shared robots.txt policy token, `__usage_ai-training`, as a cross-provider opt-out from AI training. The mechanism is defined in an IETF Internet-Draft titled "Machine-Readable Training Preference Signal", published 30 Oct and authored by Kevin Kelley of Anthropic — an individual draft, not a ratified standard; Google calls it a draft proposal and Microsoft says work continues toward a final standard. The token goes in the User-Agent line of an ordinary robots.txt group and adds no new directives. It governs training only, not crawling or product use. Implementation timelines differ: Anthropic says it has begun, OpenAI says "coming months", Microsoft targets 1 Feb 2027, Google says 2027 (its own crawler docs say early 2027). The draft states that wildcard groups (`User-Agent: *`) MUST NOT be interpreted as matching the policy token, so a site with no explicit `__usage_ai-training` group has expressed no training preference at all.

**Deliberately handled:** the post says "it is not a standard" and attributes the draft to one engineer, because that is the single most load-bearing caveat and most coverage buried it. Google's date is given as "2027" rather than "early 2027" because outlets split and Google's own docs say early 2027 while summaries say 2027 — the vaguer version is the one I can defend. The ending is the wildcard rule, which is the genuine "wait, that was the reason?" detail: the line most site owners already have does nothing here. The token string is written exactly as the draft has it, including the two leading underscores, since a typo in a post like this is the kind of thing people copy.

**Left out:** Microsoft's extra granular controls (opting out of training vs grounding separately, and sites still appearing in Bing regardless), which is true and useful but splits the post across two vendors' feature sets; Google's statement that an opt-out via the shared token without an explicit Google-Extended rule will be read as a Google-Extended disallow, which is the most technically interesting line in the whole story but needs a paragraph of its own to explain; and the AIPREF working group context.
