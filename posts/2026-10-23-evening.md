what the story is: eMudhra, an Indian certificate authority, had eight intermediate CA certificates whose P-384 private keys were generated with values below 256 — small whole numbers, recoverable by testing 256 possibilities. Per Mozilla's preliminary analysis and Scott Helme's write-up, the eight values were 4, 56, 66, 115, 155, 156, 230 and 237; Helme recovered all eight in under a second. Six of the eight are TLS intermediates, two are code signing. Timeline: the researcher flagged it on 17 Oct 2026, Chrome blocked the intermediates via CRLSet the same day, eMudhra revoked them on 18 Oct, Apple followed on 19 Oct and Microsoft on 20 Oct, and Mozilla removed the emSign ECC Root CA - G3 from its NSS trust store in 3.131.1 on 20 Oct. Chrome's root removal is scheduled for 27 Oct. On 22 Oct eMudhra's board decided to withdraw from the global SSL/TLS certificate business. The closing reveal is eMudhra's own account on the Mozilla bug tracker: a faulty script during the April 2025 key ceremony generated a second, weaker set of keys whose labels resembled the properly generated ones, so staff used the weak ones when preparing the certificates, and the issue went undetected for about 18 months through two independent audit cycles.
**Deliberately handled:** the final two beats are the company's own explanation of its own failure, which is self-serving in that it frames the cause as an accident, so the post says "by emudhra's own account" rather than presenting it as established fact. The keys were never stolen and the HSM was not the problem — this was a generation flaw, not a break-in, which is why the post never says "hacked" or "breached". Firefox is described as removing "one of emudhra's root certificates" rather than all of them, because Mozilla said on 20 Oct it was not removing trust in the G1 root for now. "last weekend" is used instead of a weekday because 17 Oct 2026 was a Saturday. eMudhra is called "an indian company" only; its Bengaluru base and listing status did not come up in search results and are not claimed. **Left out:** the ~30,000 customer certificates chaining to the G3 root that need replacing before Chrome's 27 Oct removal, and eMudhra's offer to replace them free (true and useful, but it is operational detail that competes with the ending); eMudhra's statement that only one intermediate ever reached production and none issued customer certificates; its line that it is "not aware of any evidence that the weak keys were recovered or exploited" before disclosure; and Keytos's note that eMudhra published CRLs with invalid signatures for the same six TLS intermediates in 2025 (single-sourced in my results).
